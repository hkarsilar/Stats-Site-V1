Section 3.6

Plagiarism, Authorship & Citation

Credit is the currency of research: who did the work, whose ideas these are, where the evidence came from. Most integrity failures here aren't dramatic — they're a paragraph reused without a nod, a name added as a favour, a statistic cited from a paper you never actually read. Each feels minor; each quietly misrepresents the record. This lesson covers the three places credit goes wrong — the words (plagiarism), the byline (authorship), and the sources (citation) — and then hands you six real-feeling dilemmas to work through.

Plagiarism is more than copy-paste

Plagiarism is presenting someone's words, ideas, or work as your own. It comes in more forms than students expect:

  • Verbatim. Copying text word-for-word without quotation marks and a citation — the obvious case.
  • Mosaic / patchwriting. Swapping in synonyms and shuffling a source's sentence while keeping its structure. Adding a citation doesn't rescue it: if the wording is basically theirs, quote it; if you're paraphrasing, rebuild the idea in your own structure.
  • Self-plagiarism. Reusing your own previously submitted or published work as if it were new — recycling last year's essay into your thesis, or republishing the same results. Your own words still need disclosure and, where the earlier work was assessed or published, a citation.
  • Idea theft. Taking someone's argument, design, or unpublished finding and presenting it as your own, even if not a single sentence is copied.

Paraphrase, done right: read the source, look away, restate the point in your own words and sentence shape, and cite it. Cite for the idea, quote for the wording. Both need attribution — a citation covers the idea, quotation marks cover the exact words.

Who counts as an author

Authorship means credit and accountability, so it can't be handed out as a gift or a thank-you. The widely used ICMJE standard asks for all four of these, not just one:

  • Substantial contribution to the conception, design, data, or analysis;
  • Drafting the work or revising it critically for important content;
  • Final approval of the version to be published;
  • Accountability for the work — agreeing to be answerable for its integrity.

Two failures follow directly. Gift (or honorary) authorship lists someone who didn't meet the criteria — a lab head, a friend, a favour for a CV. Ghost authorship omits someone who did — the student who ran the study, or an industry writer kept off the byline. Both distort who is responsible. People who helped but don't meet all four criteria belong in the acknowledgements, not the author list; and author order should reflect contribution, agreed openly and early (with a contributions statement, or co-first authorship when the work truly was equal).

Citation ethics

Citations are promises about where knowledge came from, so two rules matter. Cite what you actually read. If you found a 1998 statistic quoted in a 2020 review but never read the 1998 paper, don't cite the original as though you did — read it, or write "(1998, as cited in 2020 review)". Citing sources you've only seen second-hand launders unverified claims into your work and passes on any errors. And cite generously and accurately — attribute ideas to whoever had them, don't inflate your reference list with papers you didn't use, and don't strip a citation to make an idea look original.

Credit tracks contribution, not politics. The honest test for a byline is the work someone actually did, not their seniority, their feelings, or a favour owed — and the honest test for a citation is whether you truly used the source. Get both right and you're not just avoiding misconduct; you're keeping the record trustworthy for everyone who reads it next.

Why it matters: plagiarism and authorship disputes are among the most common — and most career-damaging — integrity cases students hit, and almost all of them are avoidable with habits set early: paraphrase properly and quote when in doubt, agree authorship and order at the start of a project in writing, and only ever cite what you've read. When a case is genuinely unclear, ask your supervisor or your institution's research-integrity office before you act, not after.

Common questions

Can I reuse text from my own earlier assignment?

Not by silently pasting it in — that is self-plagiarism. Reusing your own previously submitted or published work as though it were new misrepresents it, and most institutions treat re-submitting an assessed essay as an academic-integrity issue. If the earlier work was graded or published you should cite it, and you should rework the material for its new context rather than dropping identical paragraphs into a thesis; swapping a few words to disguise the reuse (patchwriting) does not help. Building on your own earlier writing is normal and fine — the honest way to do it is to disclose it, cite it, and rewrite. If you are unsure of your programme's specific rules, ask your supervisor before you reuse anything.

Does my supervisor automatically get authorship?

No — authorship is earned by contribution, not by job title. Under the widely used ICMJE criteria an author must make a substantial contribution to the work, help draft or critically revise it, approve the final version, and agree to be accountable for it. A supervisor who shaped the design, guided the analysis, and revised the manuscript clearly qualifies, and in practice many do. But merely heading the lab, providing funding, or giving occasional feedback is not enough on its own; listing someone on that basis is gift (honorary) authorship. The opposite error — dropping a student who did qualifying work — is ghost authorship. The fix for both is to agree authorship and order openly and early, based on who actually does what.

Do I have to read every paper I cite?

In effect, yes — you should cite what you have actually read. If you rely on a claim, read the source it comes from; citing a paper you have only seen quoted elsewhere, as though you read the original, is citation laundering, because you vouch for a source you never checked and can silently pass on its errors. If you genuinely cannot access an original but need to mention it, cite it honestly as a secondary source — for example '(Smith, 1998, as cited in Jones, 2020)' — which makes clear what you actually consulted. You need not read every paper cover to cover, but you should at least read the part that supports the specific point you attribute to it; abstracts can misstate a study's nuance or even the direction of its results.
