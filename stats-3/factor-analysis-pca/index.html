Section 3.5

Factor Analysis / PCA

A personality survey might ask 50 questions, but they aren't 50 independent things: answers cluster, because they tap a handful of underlying traits. Factor analysis and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) are the tools that find those few hidden dimensions hiding inside many correlated variables. They turn "50 columns" into "5 things that actually matter."

The core idea: directions of variance

PCA looks for the directions in which the data varies the most. The first principal component is the single line that captures as much of the spread as possible; the second is the best remaining direction perpendicular to the first, and so on. When variables are highly correlated, almost all the variance lines up along one direction, so one component can stand in for many.

That last readout is the one to watch. Two variables technically live in 2D, but when they're strongly correlated the cloud is really a slightly fattened line, close to one-dimensional. PCA quantifies that: if PC1 explains 95% of the variance, you can describe the data with one number per point instead of two and lose almost nothing. Scale that up from 2 variables to 50 and you have serious dimensionality reduction, which is where the same machinery reappears in machine learning.

PCA vs. factor analysis

They look similar but answer different questions. PCA is descriptive: it repackages the variance into uncorrelated components, no model assumed. Factor analysis is a model: it assumes real, unobserved latent factors (like "extraversion") that cause the observed responses, and separates shared variance from each variable's unique noise. Use PCA to compress; use factor analysis to argue that a hidden trait exists.

The model behind factor analysis

What the method rests on is a measurement model: a claim about how an observed answer comes to be, written down. It is one line. For person n answering item i:

yi,n = μi + βi ξn + εi,n

Read it left to right. An answer is where that item usually sits (μi, mu, the item's own mean), plus how strongly the item responds to the trait (βi, beta, its loading), multiplied by how much of the trait this person has (ξn, xi, their factor score), plus whatever that answer had of its own (εi,n, epsilon, the item's error).

ξ is never observed, so it arrives with no units and nothing to anchor it. Something has to fix the scale, and the convention is to standardize the factor: mean 0, variance 1. That choice is what makes every loading comparable, and it is why software reports loadings without ever asking what the factor is measured in. Standardize the items too and each β becomes the correlation between that item and the factor, which is how a loading table is normally read.

Two identities follow, and the rest of this lesson is consequences of them. The first is about two items. The errors are assumed uncorrelated with the factor and with each other, so the only route from item i to item j runs through ξ, and the trip costs one loading each way (σ, sigma, is the covariance between the two, which on standardized items is their correlation):

σij = βi βj

The second is about one item. Its variance splits into the part the factor drives and the part belonging to it alone, written ψii (psi):

σii = βi² + ψii

Three names come off that split, and they are three views of the same subtraction. βi² is the item's communality, written h²: the share of it the factor accounts for. ψii is its uniqueness, part genuinely specific content and part measurement error. And "the share of an item's variance the factor accounts for" is also what reliability means for a single indicator, so with one factor and standardized items, an item's communality is its reliability. Those two coincide because σii = 1 after standardizing; on raw, unstandardized items reliability is βi² ÷ σii and the two part company.

A worked trio makes the arithmetic concrete. Three items loading .80, .70 and .50 on one factor imply correlations of .56, .40 and .35 between them, communalities of .64, .49 and .25, and uniquenesses of .36, .51 and .75. Every pair adds back to 1, because standardizing gave each item exactly one unit of variance to divide.

One more consequence is worth stating because it explains something you will meet in print. Both identities are statements about covariances, and a correlation is a covariance divided by the two standard deviations, so the model has nothing to say about the item means: μi drops out the moment you center. A factor analysis therefore runs on the p × p matrix of correlations rather than on the N × p raw data. That is why an article can print its correlation table and let you reproduce the whole analysis, and why SPSS will run one from a matrix typed in by hand.

Is the matrix worth factoring at all?

Before any of this, software asks whether your variables are correlated enough to have shared structure to find. Two screens come as standard, and both are worth understanding rather than ticking.

KMO (the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy) compares the ordinary correlations between your variables with their partial correlations, the ones left after every other variable has been held constant. Shared structure means the plain correlations survive that adjustment and KMO climbs toward 1; variables that only correlate in unrelated pairs push it down. Kaiser's labels run from marvelous above .90 through middling in the .70s to unacceptable below .50, and .60 is the usual working floor. A clean two-factor dataset scores around .91; pure noise scores about .50.

Bartlett's test of sphericity asks a much weaker question: could your correlation matrix have come from variables that are all mutually uncorrelated? Treat a significant result as a formality rather than an achievement. With 20 variables and 100 people, true correlations of just .10 already give p = .00005, and at 300 people the same tiny correlations give p below 10⁻¹⁶. A non-significant Bartlett means stop; a significant one means almost nothing on its own, so read KMO and the scree plot for the real answer.

Cronbach's α (alpha) is usually run in the same session and answers a third question, which is not the one the other two ask. KMO and Bartlett ask whether the matrix has shared structure worth looking for. α assumes you already believe these items belong together and asks how consistently they behave as one scale:

α = (k / (k − 1)) × (1 − Σσii ÷ σ²total)

k is the number of items, σii each item's variance and σ²total the variance of the total score. The ratio inside the bracket is the share of total-score variance the items carry separately; what is left over is the share they carry together, which is the quantity α is reporting. A working floor of .70 is conventional, .80 is comfortable for research use, and above about .95 is a warning rather than a triumph, since it usually means several items are near-duplicates of each other. The eight wellbeing items score .90.

Two honest caveats travel with that number, and both matter when a reviewer quotes it back at you.

The first is that α climbs with the number of items whatever those items are worth. Hold the average correlation between items at a mediocre .20 and simply keep adding more of the same:

Items k48122030

Nothing about the items improved along that row. Thirty items correlating .20 with each other post a respectable .88, which is the same reliability logic the Spearman-Brown formula states directly: averaging more indicators cancels more item-specific noise. Report α beside k, and read a high α on a long scale as the arithmetic doing its job rather than as evidence the items are good.

The second is that α is about internal consistency, not about unidimensionality. It never asks how many factors there are. A questionnaire built from two clearly separate factors whose items correlate reasonably within each block can still return an α in the .80s, so a comfortable α is no defense against a scree plot that says two. Use α to report how consistently a scale you have already established behaves, and use the factor analysis to establish it.

Choosing an extraction method

Extraction is the step that turns a correlation matrix into loadings, and software offers a menu of ways to do it. Two of them account for nearly everything you will read.

Principal axis factoring takes the identities above literally. It puts an estimate of each item's communality on the diagonal of the correlation matrix in place of the 1s, solves for the loadings, uses those loadings to re-estimate the communalities, puts the new values back on the diagonal, and repeats until they stop moving. What it is converging on is the flattest residual grid the widget above can be pushed to. It assumes nothing about the shape of the data, which is the reason to choose it: Likert answers are ordinal, often skewed, and rarely normal by any test you would care to run.

Maximum likelihood asks a different question. Treating the variables as multivariate normal, which loadings make the observed data most likely? That assumption is a real cost, and it buys something principal axis factoring cannot offer: a likelihood, and with it a formal goodness-of-fit test of whether the number of factors you asked for is enough. The worked χ² further down this page came from maximum likelihood for exactly that reason, and no amount of iterating a principal-axis solution would have produced it.

So: reach for principal axis factoring when normality is doubtful and what you want is a defensible structure, and for maximum likelihood when the items are plausibly continuous and roughly normal and you want to test a specific number of factors. In SPSS both sit in one dropdown under Extraction…, and the default there is neither of them, so it is a box worth opening rather than accepting.

How many factors to keep

An eigenvalue usually arrives as "the variance a factor accounts for", which is true and explains nothing. Said in the loadings you already have, it is a sum: a factor's eigenvalue is the total of its squared loadings down the column. Each βi² is the slice of item i that the factor explains, so adding them across the items counts how many whole items' worth of variance that factor is carrying. The eight wellbeing items above have squared loadings running from .444 to .575, and they total 4.32. One factor is doing the work of four and a third of the eight, which is 54% of the eight units of variance there are to explain.

Read that way, the Kaiser criterion (keep factors with an eigenvalue above 1) stops being an arbitrary threshold. Every item was standardized, which leaves it one unit of variance to account for, so 1 is what a single item is worth. A factor whose eigenvalue falls below it explains less than one of your own questions does, and you would be better off keeping the question. The rule says: earn your place against one item.

The scree plot draws those eigenvalues in descending order and asks you to find the "elbow" where the line flattens. A third habit is to keep enough factors to reach a target share of the variance, often 80 or 90% in a components analysis and rather less in a factor analysis, which never claimed the unique variance to begin with.

Parallel analysis asks the same question against a fairer benchmark, and it is the retention rule most methodologists now recommend: simulate many random datasets with your number of variables and your number of people, average their eigenvalues, and keep only the factors whose real eigenvalue beats the matching random one. That comparison exists because 1 is a low bar. With 12 variables and 100 respondents of pure noise, the largest eigenvalue averages 1.61, and on average 5.6 of the 12 clear 1.0, so the Kaiser criterion happily keeps factors built entirely from sampling variation.

Expect the rules to disagree. Scree and Kaiser routinely return different counts on the same matrix, and parallel analysis lands somewhere between them often enough that a student who was promised one answer feels cheated. There is no third statistic that settles it, and reaching for one is the wrong move. The tie-break is interpretability and theory: fit the candidate counts, rotate each, and keep the solution whose factors you can name and defend to someone who knows the construct. A two-factor solution you can explain beats a three-factor solution where the third factor is two items and a shrug.

Does the model fit?

Maximum likelihood extraction earns its normality assumption by handing back a formal test, and the test is worth reading carefully because its null hypothesis runs the opposite way to almost every other one on this site. It says that m factors are enough: that the model-implied correlations are the population's own, and every residual you can see is sampling noise. So a small p is bad news here. You are hoping for a result you cannot reject.

The degrees of freedom come from counting. A correlation matrix over p items holds p(p + 1) ÷ 2 distinct numbers. The model spends pm of them on loadings and p more on uniquenesses, then hands back m(m − 1) ÷ 2, because rotation makes that many of the loadings redundant. Subtract and collect terms:

df = ((p − m)² − (p + m)) ÷ 2

On the eight wellbeing items, that runs down fast:

The 36 numbers in an eight-item correlation matrix minus 16 parameters gives the 20 the page's own χ²(20) = 20.14 is read against, and p = .45 says one factor reproduces those correlations as well as sampling noise would allow. Three factors leave only seven. Keep going and the model eventually has as many parameters as the matrix has numbers, at which point it fits perfectly and says nothing at all.

Two cautions travel with the test. It exists only under maximum likelihood, so a principal-axis solution has no fit statistic and a write-up quoting one alongside "principal axis factoring" has quoted somebody else's output. And χ² scales with sample size, which this page can demonstrate on itself. The one-factor solution above reproduces its correlations so well that the largest residual is −.024; the same discrepancy, measured on 200 people instead of 75, would return χ²(20) = 56.19 and p < .001, and on 1,000 people χ²(20) = 286.91. Nothing about the fit changed. This is the same trap as a normality test rejecting a harmless departure in a large sample, and the answer is the same: read the test beside the residual grid rather than instead of it. A significant χ² with every residual under .05 is a large-sample artifact; a significant χ² with three residuals past .15 is the model telling you something.

Rotation: the same model, made readable

Extraction hands back a solution nobody can name. The first factor comes out as a general factor that every item loads on, because the algorithm is told to take as much variance as it can before moving on, and the second is left mopping up whatever separates the items, which makes it bipolar: half the items positive, half negative. That is an accurate description of the data and a useless description of the constructs. Rotation is the step that turns one into the other.

It is allowed because a factor solution is not unique, and the algebra of that is two lines. Write Λ (a capital lambda) for the matrix of loadings. Everything the model claims about the data is in Λ ΛT, the grid of implied correlations from the section above. Now take any orthogonal matrix T, one whose own transpose is its inverse, and rotate the loadings with it:

(ΛT)(ΛT)T = Λ T TT ΛT = Λ ΛT

The T and its inverse meet in the middle and cancel. Every implied correlation, every communality, every residual and the total variance explained come out identical, while the loading table can be made to look completely different. Allow T to be any invertible matrix rather than an orthogonal one and the same cancellation still works, provided the factors are now allowed to correlate: put Λ* = ΛT⁻¹ and let Φ (phi, the matrix of correlations between the factors) be TTT, and Λ* Φ Λ*T is Λ ΛT again. That is the entire justification for rotation, and it is also its limit. Rotation cannot improve a solution, because there is nothing there to improve; it can only make one version of it easier to talk about.

Which leaves a choice about Φ, and the choice is a claim about your constructs rather than a setting. Orthogonal rotation, of which varimax is the one everyone uses, holds Φ at the identity: the factors are forced to be uncorrelated. Oblique rotation, whose common forms are promax and direct oblimin, lets them correlate and then reports how much. In psychology the oblique family is usually the honest default, because a theory that posits two related constructs is not claiming they are independent, and an oblique rotation will tell you what the correlation actually came out at. Run oblimin first and read Φ: if the factors come out near zero, varimax was defensible after all and you now have evidence for it rather than an assumption.

Pattern or structure: which table do I read?

After an orthogonal rotation there is one rotated factor matrix and no ambiguity. After an oblique one, SPSS prints two, and they are not the same numbers.

The pattern matrix holds each factor's own contribution to an item, with the other factors held constant, which makes a pattern coefficient a regression weight rather than a correlation. The structure matrix holds plain correlations between items and factors, and when the factors are correlated those correlations are inflated: an item tied to factor 2 will correlate respectably with factor 1 too, purely because the factors travel together. Interpret the pattern matrix. This is the single most common misreading of an oblique solution, and the widget above makes it concrete. Switch it to oblique, press the button, and it settles at −32° with the factors correlating .40. Read the pattern columns and seven items load on exactly one factor. Read the structure columns, on the same solution and the same data, and only four do: i1, i5 and i7 each pick up a second loading past .32 whose pattern coefficient is .002, .048 and .076. Three cross-loadings appear out of nothing but the correlation between the factors.

One arithmetic consequence catches people out. Under an orthogonal rotation an item's communality is the sum of its squared loadings, and under an oblique one it is not, because the factors overlap and the overlap would be double-counted. The communality column stays where it was, since rotation cannot move it, but you can no longer reconstruct it by squaring and adding the pattern coefficients.

Reading the loading table

Two thresholds do most of the work, and both have a reason underneath them. A loading of .32 is the usual floor for an item counting toward a factor, because .32² = .1024, so the factor is explaining about a tenth of that item's variance, which is the least that is worth writing down. A loading of .50 squares to .25 and is the conventional mark of a solid indicator, a quarter of the item accounted for. Some fields use .40 as the floor instead; what matters is stating which you used before you apply it, rather than picking the one that tidies your table.

Reverse-code negatively worded items before the analysis, not after. On a 1 to 5 agreement scale the reversed score is 6 − x, so 1 becomes 5, 2 becomes 4, 3 stays put, and the general form on a scale running 1 to k is (k + 1) − x. Skip the step and the item correlates negatively with everything else in its block, which does not merely flip a sign in the output: extraction sees a group of items that fail to hang together and can hand back a second factor made entirely of the reversed items. The wellbeing data on this page has two such items, q3 and q6, and both are reverse-coded before anything else happens. Recode into a new variable rather than over the original, so the raw file survives the mistake you have not made yet.

A cross-loading is a two-part test, and the second part is the one people forget. An item cross-loads when it loads above the threshold on two factors and those two loadings sit within about .20 of each other. Item i4 in the widget is the live example: after varimax it reads .589 and .485, both past .32 and .104 apart, so it belongs to neither factor in any useful sense. An item reading .62 and .35 is a different animal. It is over the threshold twice, but it is plainly a factor-1 item with a secondary echo, and the write-up can say so.

When an item does cross-load, work through the remedies in this order rather than reaching for the last one first.

  1. Re-specify the number of factors. A cross-loading is often a symptom of a count that is wrong by one, and the item lands cleanly when the right number is fitted.
  2. Try the other rotation. An item straddling two correlated factors can resolve under an oblique rotation that an orthogonal one was forcing to share.
  3. Drop the item, and say in the write-up that you did and why. This is a real option and it is third rather than first, because a dropped item cannot be got back and the information it carried goes with it.
  4. Check what is left. Each factor wants at least three indicators to be stable; two is a fragile factor and one is not a factor at all. If dropping the item takes a factor down to two, the problem was the questionnaire rather than the item.

Then name the factors, which is the part no statistic helps with. Read the items loading highest on each and write a label that a colleague could predict from the items alone. A factor you can only name by describing its loadings has not been interpreted.

How much of an item does the model explain?

With one factor the split above is all there is: h² is βi² and the uniqueness is what is left. With several factors the communality is the sum of an item's squared loadings across the ones you kept, and the arithmetic is otherwise unchanged. An item loading .70 on a single factor has h² = .49, so the model explains just under half of it.

Fit a one-factor model to the eight wellbeing items in wellbeing-survey.csv (after reverse-coding q3 and q6) and the loadings land between .67 and .76, with communalities from .44 to .58 and a mean of .54. One factor is enough: χ²(20) = 20.14, p = .45. Those same eight items have a Cronbach's α of .90. Both numbers are correct, and reading them together is the useful part: any single item is about half noise, while their average is not, because the item-specific halves are unrelated to each other and largely cancel. That is the argument for scoring a scale instead of asking one good question, and it is reliability viewed from the item's side.

Communality is also where the two methods part company mechanically. PCA analyzes the correlation matrix with 1s down the diagonal, treating every variable as fully explainable and repackaging all of the variance, error included. Factor analysis puts estimated communalities on that diagonal instead, so it models only the variance the items share. With strong items the two solutions look almost identical; with weak ones PCA reports larger loadings and more variance explained than the data can support.

Two habits follow. Read the communality column before dropping an item for a low loading, because an item can load modestly on each of three correlated factors and still be well explained. And treat a communality near .20 as a verdict on the item rather than on the model: whatever that item is measuring, the factors you kept do not contain it. If the items came from a questionnaire, the fix usually lives back in how the item was worded, not in the extraction settings.

Factor scores

Sooner or later you want a number per person rather than a number per item: a wellbeing score to correlate with something else, or to put on the right-hand side of a regression. That number is a factor score, and the careful word for what you do to get one is predict rather than compute or estimate. The distinction is worth keeping. You compute a quantity that is fully determined by the data, like a mean. You estimate a parameter, a fixed unknown constant of the population, like a loading. A person's ξn is neither: it is a real, realized quantity that belongs to that person and simply was not observed, in the same family as a residual in a regression, which is also predicted rather than estimated.

Because it was never observed, there is no single right answer, and software offers a choice of predictors. Two of them cover what you will meet.

The regression predictor (SPSS calls it the regression method, and it is the default) picks the weights that minimize the mean squared distance between the predicted scores and the true ones. It is the most accurate predictor available in that sense, and it takes the correlations between factors into account, which is what you want after an oblique rotation. The cost is that its scores are not clean: a score for factor 1 correlates somewhat with factor 2 as well, so two factor scores from a correlated pair come out more correlated than the factors themselves are.

The Bartlett predictor takes the other trade. Its scores are unbiased, meaning that across repeated samples they center on the true factor score rather than shrinking toward the mean, and each score stays correlated with its own factor and not the others. That second property is the one to reach for when the scores themselves are the outcome of interest and you need to be able to say that this column measures this factor and nothing else.

So: after an orthogonal rotation either is defensible, and the question is which you care about more, accuracy or unbiasedness. After an oblique rotation the regression predictor is the usual choice, because it is the one that knows the factors are correlated. Say which one you used, since a reader cannot tell from the column of numbers and the two do not agree.

One habit worth having whichever you pick: a plain sum or average of the items usually correlates above .95 with the predicted scores, travels between samples without re-fitting, and is what most published scales actually report. On the eight wellbeing items on this page the two agree at r = .999, which is what a scale of similar loadings will normally give you. Predicted scores earn their keep when the loadings are genuinely uneven or the factors correlate; they are not automatically better than adding the items up.

The order you do it in

Six steps, in the order a practical exam will ask for them, with the section of this page that covers each.

  1. Explore the data first. Reverse-code the negatively worded items, look at the correlation matrix, and run KMO and Bartlett's test to check there is shared structure to find at all.
  2. Decide how many factors. Scree, Kaiser and parallel analysis, with theory breaking the tie and the fit test available if you extracted by maximum likelihood.
  3. Estimate the loadings, naming the extraction method and why: principal axis factoring when normality is doubtful, maximum likelihood when it is not and you want the fit test.
  4. Rotate. Orthogonal if you are claiming independent constructs, oblique if you are not, and read Φ to find out which claim the data supports.
  5. Interpret and label. Apply your stated loading threshold, deal with the cross-loadings, check each factor still has three indicators, and give the factors names.
  6. Predict factor scores, if you need a number per person for what comes next.

Steps 2 and 3 are not as sequential as the list makes them look. Fitting two candidate counts and comparing the rotated solutions is normal practice rather than cheating, provided you say that is what you did.

Why it matters: PCA and factor analysis are everywhere data is wide: psychometrics, genomics, image compression, recommender systems. They're how you find structure and signal when you have far more variables than you can reason about one at a time. In psychometrics especially they sit right next to reliability and validity: a factor structure is the evidence that a scale measures the one thing it claims to.

Problem 81 of the practice problems hands you three loadings and the correlations the three items actually produced, and asks for the implied matrix, the communalities and the residuals, then what happens when you refit one loading. Problem 82 is the α caveat as arithmetic: a six-item scale, the same six items written twice over, and the reliability coefficient that climbs without a single item improving. Problem 83 takes a three-factor extraction apart with eigenvalues and the fit test, including the factor that turns out to be worth a sixth of one item, and Problem 84 is a rotated oblique solution to label, with a structure matrix that makes every item look like a cross-loading and one item that genuinely is.

Common questions

My supervisor says 10 participants per item. Is that the rule?

It is one of two rules you will be handed, the other being a flat 300. Simulating the thing settles it better, and what matters most is neither: it is how strong your items are. Twelve items in three factors, 1,000 replications per cell, comparing the recovered loadings against the ones the data were built from. With strong items (loadings of .80, so communalities of .64) the average loading is off by .086 at N = 60, .064 at N = 100, and .037 at N = 300. With weak items (loadings of .50) the same design is off by .184 at N = 60 and still .067 at N = 300. So 100 people answering strong items recover the structure about as well as 300 answering weak ones, while the 10-per-item rule would have waved through the weak study at N = 120 (off by .118) and blocked the strong one at N = 100. Ask about the items first: several clear indicators per factor with high communalities buy you a small sample, and noisy items cannot be rescued by recruiting.

Do I have to rotate at all?

With one factor there is nothing to rotate: a single axis can be turned only onto itself, which is why a one-factor solution never comes with a rotated table. With two or more, rotation is close to universal, and the reason is what extraction leaves behind rather than any statistical gain. The first factor is built to take as much variance as it can, so nearly every item loads on it, and the ones after it are left describing whatever separates the items, which makes them bipolar. That is a fair account of the variance and a hopeless account of the constructs. Since rotation changes neither the fit nor the communalities nor a single implied correlation, declining to rotate buys nothing and costs you the ability to name what you found. The one place unrotated loadings are read is when the first factor is itself the object of interest, as in a general-intelligence or general-distress argument.

Can I just add up the items instead of saving factor scores?

Usually, yes, and most published scales do exactly that. A unit-weighted sum or average typically correlates above .95 with the predicted factor scores, travels to a new sample without refitting anything, and can be compared with other studies using the same instrument, which a factor score cannot: a factor score is standardized inside your own dataset, so a value of 0.4 means nothing outside it. Predicted scores earn their keep when the loadings are genuinely uneven, when the factors correlate and you want the overlap handled, or when a later model needs the factor rather than a proxy for it. If you do sum, reverse-code first, say whether you summed or averaged, and report the α of the items you added together.
