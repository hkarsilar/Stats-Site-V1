Probability Basics

Probability puts a number on uncertainty. A probability is a value between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain) that says how likely something is. The most useful way to think of it is as a long-run frequency: how often the event would happen if you could repeat the situation over and over.

Probability as long-run frequency

Say a coin has a 0.5 chance of heads. That doesn't promise heads on any particular flip, or even 5 heads in 10. It's a statement about the long run: flip it enough times and the proportion of heads will close in on 0.5. This is the Law of Large Numbers, and it connects abstract probability to actual data. A related result comes later: the law says where the proportion settles, and the Central Limit Theorem describes the shape of its fluctuations along the way.

Sample space and events

Before you apply any rule, list the possible outcomes. The sample space is every outcome the random phenomenon can produce, written S. Roll one die and S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Flip two coins and S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}. That is four outcomes, not three, because HT and TH are different results that happen to give the same count of heads. An event is any collection of outcomes you care about. "Even" is the event {2, 4, 6}; "at least one head" is {HH, HT, TH}.

When every outcome in S is equally likely, probability becomes counting. Count the outcomes in the event, divide by the count in S, and that is the answer: P(even) = 3/6 = .5. Most of the work in an exam question is building S correctly, and most of the mistakes happen there too. Two coins have four outcomes, not three. Two dice have 36, not 21.

Four rules to start from

  • Range. Every probability is between 0 and 1.
  • Total. The probabilities of all the outcomes in S add to exactly 1.
  • Complement. P(not A) = 1 − P(A). The chance of not rolling a 6 is 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.
  • Addition for disjoint events. If two events cannot both happen, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Rolling a 1 or a 2 is 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

Events that cannot both happen are called disjoint, or mutually exclusive; the two words mean the same thing and both turn up in exam papers. The complement rule looks too simple to matter, but it is one of the most useful rules on this page, because a question about "at least one" is nearly always easier to answer from the other end. Work out the probability that it never happens, then subtract from 1. That step solves the first of the three classic problems below.

When events overlap: the general addition rule

The addition rule above has a condition: it fails as soon as two events can happen together. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts everything in the overlap twice, once inside each total, so you subtract the overlap once:

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

The rest of this lesson uses one example. A firm asked every one of its 200 employees how they get to work, then sorted the replies by whether the person is under 30.

For disjoint events the overlap is empty, so the shorter rule works for them and only for them.

Independence and the multiplication rule

Two events are independent when knowing that one happened does not change the probability of the other. Separate coin flips qualify. So do two draws from a deck, provided you put the first card back. For independent events, probabilities multiply:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

Two fair coins both landing heads is ½ × ½ = ¼. Three sixes in a row is (1/6)³ = 1/216. The rule extends to as many independent events as you like. Each extra event multiplies in another factor below 1, so a long streak is far rarer than a short one.

Watch out for "independent." Multiplying works only when the events don't affect each other. Drawing cards without replacement, or events that share a common cause, break independence, and most real-world probability mistakes come from assuming it when it doesn't hold.

The addition and multiplication rules together explain why 7 is the most common total with two dice. Two dice have 36 equally likely outcomes, and the eleven possible totals do not share them evenly: one combination makes 2, and six make 7. Counting them all out gives a triangular distribution, which you can build a die at a time in the distribution playground and watch turn into a bell.

Conditional probability

Events that do affect each other need a new symbol. P(A | B), read "the probability of A given B", asks a narrower question than P(A). P(A) asks how common A is among everyone; P(A | B) asks how common it is among only the people who are also B. The definition divides one probability by another:

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Conditioning discards every row of the table that fails the condition, so the denominator shrinks to that row's own total rather than staying at 200. In the survey, P(cycles) = 70/200 = .35 across the whole company, while P(cycles | under 30) = 48/100 = .48. The gap between those two numbers is an association, and much of the rest of Stats 1 and Stats 2 is about testing associations like it. Independence is the special case where the gap is zero, so that P(A | B) = P(A) and learning B tells you nothing about A.

Rearranging the definition gives the general multiplication rule, which holds whether or not the events are independent:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

On the table, P(under 30 and cycles) = .50 × .48 = .24, matching the 48 of 200 you can read off directly. When the events are independent, P(B | A) equals P(B) and the general rule reduces to the short one. Cards dealt without replacement are the standard case where it does not: P(two aces) = (4/52) × (3/51) = 1/221, because the second probability depends on what the first draw removed.

Turning it around: Bayes' rule

P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers, and confusing them is one of the most costly mistakes in applied probability. A screening test that catches 99% of real cases has told you P(positive | ill). The person holding the positive result wants P(ill | positive). Bayes' rule converts one into the other:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)

The numerator is the general multiplication rule read backwards. The denominator is the part that usually needs work, since P(B) is rarely given directly. It comes from the law of total probability: an event can be reached along several disjoint routes, and its overall probability is the sum of the probabilities of those routes.

P(B) = P(B | A) × P(A) + P(B | not A) × P(not A)

Positive results come from two routes: ill people the test catches, and healthy people it flags anyway. Together they make up every positive result. On the survey table, the same sum gives a number you can also read straight off it: P(cycles) = .48 × .50 + .22 × .50 = .35. Bayes' rule then answers the reversed question, P(under 30 | cycles) = .48 × .50 / .35 = .686, which matches the 48 of 70 cyclists you can count in the first column.

At the defaults the tree shows a good test, right 99% of the time in both directions, applied to a condition that affects 1 person in 1,000. Of 100,000 people it correctly flags 99 of the 100 who are ill. It also flags 999 of the 99,900 who are not, so barely 9% of the flagged group is ill. The test is working as designed. False positives outnumber true ones because there are a thousand times more healthy people than ill ones. So screening programs report positive predictive value (the share of positives who are ill) rather than accuracy.

Raise the prevalence and the same test's positive results become reliable: at 1 in 10, P(ill | positive) reaches 91.7%, and at 1 in 5 it reaches 96.1%. The test has not changed, only the population it is applied to. That is the argument for screening the people at risk rather than everybody. Bayesian reasoning goes much further than one 2 × 2 table, and Bayesian thinking in Stats 3 picks it up as a way of doing inference.

Three classic problems

The Chevalier's two bets. Antoine Gombaud, a seventeenth-century writer with a taste for gambling, bet on at least one six in four throws of a die and did well. He then bet on at least one double six in twenty-four throws of two dice and did badly. He expected the bets to behave alike, since 24 is to 36 as 4 is to 6. The complement rule explains the difference. P(at least one six in 4 throws) = 1 − (5/6)⁴ = .5177, a favorable bet; P(at least one double six in 24 throws) = 1 − (35/36)²⁴ = .4914, an unfavorable one. The difference is about 3 percentage points, quite enough to notice over a long season. The correspondence between Pascal and Fermat that his question set off in 1654 is where a good deal of probability theory begins.

The birthday problem. In a room of 23 people, the probability that at least two share a birthday is .5073, near enough a coin flip. The complement rule again: the probability that all 23 birthdays differ is (365/365) × (364/365) × … × (343/365) = .4927, and 1 minus that is the answer. Intuition fails because it answers a different question. The question is not whether someone shares your birthday, which means only 22 comparisons. It is whether any pair matches, and 23 people make C(23, 2) = 253 pairs. With 50 people the probability rises to .9704.

The prosecutor's fallacy. P(the evidence | innocent) is not P(innocent | the evidence), and a court that treats them as the same number can reach the wrong answer with confidence. Suppose one person in a million matches some forensic description. That is P(matches | innocent). On its own it says nothing about P(innocent | matches), which also depends on how many people could have matched in the first place; in a city of five million, several innocent people will. Two convictions later overturned on appeal, those of Sally Clark in England and Lucia de Berk in the Netherlands, rested in part on probability arguments that statisticians criticized on these grounds. In the Clark case the Royal Statistical Society wrote publicly about the evidence heard at trial. The same reversal of a conditional underlies the replication crisis: a p-value reports P(data this extreme | no effect), but the rate at which published findings are false positives is a different and larger number.

Why it matters: every p-value, confidence level, and sampling distribution is a probability statement. "5% of intervals miss" and "p < 0.05" only mean something because of the long-run frequency idea at the start of this lesson.

Common questions

What is the difference between independent and mutually exclusive events?

Independent events don't influence each other: knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other (two separate coin flips), and P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Mutually exclusive events can't both happen (one die roll can't be both a 1 and a 2), so P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). They're near-opposites: mutually exclusive events are maximally dependent, since one happening rules the other out.

What is the gambler's fallacy?

The belief that after a run of heads, tails is "due." It isn't — each flip is independent, and the coin has no memory. The Law of Large Numbers says the long-run proportion converges to the truth; it never promises short-run correction. The lopsided early flips just get diluted by the thousands that follow.

How do I tell whether a question wants me to add or to multiply?

Read the connector first. An "or" question asks for a union and points at addition; an "and" question asks for two things happening together and points at multiplication. Then check which version of the rule applies. For "or", the short form P(A) + P(B) is valid only when the two events cannot both occur; otherwise you must subtract the overlap. For "and", the short form P(A) × P(B) is valid only when the events are independent; otherwise the second factor is P(B | A). Two habits catch most of the errors. If an answer comes out above 1, an overlap was counted twice somewhere. And "at least one" is an "or" question in disguise, nearly always quicker through the complement: work out the probability that it never happens, which is a multiplication, then subtract from 1.
