Section 1.12

Hypothesis Testing Logic

A hypothesis test is a structured way to answer "could this result just be a fluke?" The logic mirrors a courtroom: the null hypothesis is presumed innocent, and we only convict (reject it) when the evidence would be surprising if it were true.

The four moves

  1. State two hypotheses. The null (H₀) is the boring "nothing's going on" claim (no effect, no difference). The alternative (H₁) is what you suspect instead. (Turning a vague research question into these two crisp statements is a skill of its own. From Question to Hypothesis walks the whole path.)
  2. Assume H₀ is true. This gives you a null distribution, the range of results you'd expect from chance alone if there were no effect.
  3. Measure surprise. Find where your actual result falls on that distribution. The p-value is the probability of getting a result this extreme or more, just by chance, if H₀ were true.
  4. Decide. If the p-value is below a pre-set threshold α (usually 0.05), the result is "too surprising to be chance," so you reject H₀. Otherwise, fail to reject it.

Where the statistic comes from

The explorer below starts from a z, because this lesson is about the logic. An exam gives you a claim and a sample, and the first job is to turn them into that single number. When the population standard deviation σ (sigma) is known, the arithmetic is short. Take the gap between what you saw and what the claim predicts, then measure that gap in standard errors:

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)

μ₀ (mu-nought) is the value the null hypothesis names, and the question gives it to you. The denominator is the standard error, the usual sample-to-sample variation of a mean. Dividing by it puts z on the same scale whatever the units of measurement: z is the number of standard errors between the sample mean and the claimed value.

What a p-value is — and isn't

Slide the statistic out toward the tails and the p-value shrinks: extreme results are unlikely under chance, so they count as evidence against H₀. But the p-value is easy to misread, and two misreadings are common:

A p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true. It's the probability of data this extreme assuming H₀ is true. Those are different questions. p = 0.03 does not mean "3% chance there's no effect."

"Fail to reject" is NOT "proven true." A courtroom's "not guilty" doesn't mean "definitely innocent," only "not enough evidence." Likewise, a non-significant result means we couldn't rule out chance, not that H₀ is correct. (How to write that up without calling it a "trend toward significance" is covered in Writing About Non-Significant Results.)

The five steps, in exam order

The four moves above are the logic. On paper, an examiner wants them written in a fixed order with the arithmetic in the middle, and marks are given for the steps rather than the answer:

  1. Write H₀ and H₁ as statements about the population parameter, in symbols, before looking at the data. H₀ names one value; H₁ says what "not that value" means for this question.
  2. Fix α. It is a decision about how much risk of a false alarm you will accept, and you make it here, before you see the p-value.
  3. Compute the test statistic, showing the substitution as well as the result.
  4. Find the p-value from a table or a calculator, matching the tail or tails to H₁.
  5. Compare p with α and write the conclusion in the words of the original question, not as "reject H₀" alone.

The five steps on a cereal line. A filling machine is set to 227 g a box, and the factory's records give σ = 5 g. Four boxes off this morning's line average 222 g. (1) H₀: μ = 227 against H₁: μ ≠ 227, since "has the machine drifted" does not say which way. (2) α = .05. (3) z = (222 − 227) / (5/√4) = −5 / 2.5 = −2.00. (4) Two-sided, so p = 2 × P(Z < −2.00) = .0455. (5) Because .0455 is below .05, reject H₀: this morning's boxes are lighter than the machine is meant to make them. At α = .01 the same evidence would not be enough, so the report should say which α was used. The 95% interval built from these numbers was [217.1, 226.9], and it excludes 227. At a matching α, a two-sided test and a confidence interval always agree.

One tail or two, and where the choice comes from

The research question decides the explorer's alternative setting, and it is decided before any data arrive.

Wording that names a direction points at one tail: "is the new fertilizer better", "does the drug lower blood pressure", H₁: μ > μ₀ or H₁: μ < μ₀. Wording that only asks whether something has moved points at both: "is the machine off target", "is the printed label incorrect", "do the two groups differ", H₁: μ ≠ μ₀. Two-sided is the cautious default, and software reports it unless you ask for one tail.

Which one you picked also changes how the p-value is read off a printed table. A left-tailed test reads the area below your z straight from Table A. A right-tailed test reads that area and subtracts it from 1, or equivalently looks up −z and reads the area below that. A two-sided test finds one tail and doubles it.

Picking the tail after seeing which way the result went is cheating. It halves the p-value and raises the true false-alarm rate from 5% to 10%. For that reason it appears on every list of questionable research practices. If the direction was not predicted in advance, the test is two-sided. A one-tailed test commits you in advance to treating a result in the other direction, however large, as non-significant.

Significant, and possibly trivial

A small p-value says the data would be surprising if nothing were going on. It says nothing about how much is going on. Sample size separates the two questions: with enough data, a difference far too small to matter will clear any threshold you like.

Picture a weight-loss app tested on forty thousand users, reporting a mean loss 0.2 kg greater than the control group's at p < .001. The difference is almost certainly real, and it is a fifth of a kilogram. Statistical significance is a claim about evidence; practical significance is a claim about size, and only the second one answers "should anyone change what they do?"

To keep them apart, report an effect size and a confidence interval beside every p-value. Then the reader of your paper sees a size and its uncertainty as well as the verdict. If a paper reports an effect size you are not used to, the effect-size converter will convert it to one you know.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence

"Study finds no link between X and Y" is a headline written off a p-value above .05, and it almost always claims more than the study showed. Failing to reject H₀ means the data were compatible with no effect. They were also compatible with a small effect, and frequently with a large one, because a test that fails to reject has not measured anything precisely.

The confidence interval tells these cases apart. For a difference, an interval of [−0.1, +0.2] does rule out anything big. An interval of [−4.0, +4.5] rules out nothing at all and reports only that the study was too small to tell. Both results are non-significant, but only the first supports the headline.

Writing About Non-Significant Results covers how to write this up without overclaiming in either direction. It also covers equivalence testing, the procedure to use when "no meaningful difference" is the claim you set out to make.

Two ways to be wrong, and how often

A test can be wrong in two ways. A Type I error rejects a true H₀, reporting an effect that was never there, and its rate is exactly α. So α is the one error rate you choose directly: set it at .05 and you will raise a false alarm on 5% of the occasions when there is no effect. A Type II error misses an effect that is present, and its rate is written β (beta). You do not set β directly. It depends on three things: how big the real effect is, how variable the measurement is, and how much data you gathered.

Studies usually report β's complement. The power of a test, 1 − β, is the probability of detecting an effect of a stated size when that effect exists. Both quantities are easier to see when you draw two sampling distributions on one axis: the one H₀ predicts, and the one the true mean would produce.

A nursery's seedlings have averaged 22.0 cm at four weeks for years, with σ = 0.5 cm. A new light schedule might raise the mean to 22.6. Testing five trays at α = .05, two-sided, the cutoffs are 22.0 ± 1.960 × 0.5/√5, which is 22.0 ± 0.438, so the test rejects for a tray mean below 21.562 or above 22.438. If the new mean is 22.6, the probability that a tray mean is beyond that upper cutoff is .765. With a power of .765, a study designed this way misses a real effect roughly one time in four.

With the two curves in view, each control does what you would expect. More data narrows both curves and reduces their overlap. A larger true effect moves them further apart. A smaller α pushes the cutoffs outward and lowers power. The two error rates trade against each other, and no α setting shrinks both at once. A power analysis chooses n, before any data exist, so that power reaches .80. The power calculator runs one, and Effect Size & Power teaches it in full.

Why it matters: every test you'll meet (t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square, regression) follows these same four moves. Learn the logic here, and each later test differs mainly in its test statistic and its null distribution.

Common questions

What does p < 0.05 actually mean?

It means: if there were no effect (H₀ true), data this extreme would occur less than 5% of the time by chance alone. Since that's rare, we treat the result as evidence against H₀. It does not mean there's a 95% chance the effect is real, and it says nothing about how large or important the effect is. That's the job of effect sizes.

Why learn the z-test when σ is almost never known?

It usually isn't, and §1.13 replaces σ with the sample's own s. The z version is still taught first because it shows one idea at a time. With σ known, the mean is the only thing being estimated, the sampling distribution is exactly normal, and you can follow every step of the logic without the extra step of estimating a spread. It is also less artificial than it sounds. A long-running measurement process (a calibrated instrument, an assay a lab has run for years, a standardized test whose SD is published) does have a known spread, while its mean is the thing in question. The two procedures converge as n grows, because the t distribution approaches the normal as its degrees of freedom grow. So the bottom row of a printed Table D reads 1.645, 1.960 and 2.576, the same three z* values the σ-known interval uses.

Why is the significance level set at 0.05?

It is a convention. R. A. Fisher suggested in the 1920s that one in twenty was a convenient benchmark for "surprising" (roughly the chance of a value beyond ±2 SDs), and it stuck. Nothing special happens between p = .049 and p = .051, so fields with different stakes choose differently. Particle physics demands "5 sigma" (about 1 in 3.5 million), genome-wide studies use 5 × 10⁻⁸, and some journals now suggest .005 for new discoveries. What matters is fixing α before you look at the data, and remembering that crossing it says nothing about how large or important the effect is.
