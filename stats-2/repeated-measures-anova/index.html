Section 2.5

Repeated Measures ANOVA

When you measure the same people under several conditions (the same patients before, during, and after treatment), you have a repeated-measures design. Just as the paired t-test beats the independent one, repeated-measures ANOVA has a superpower: it can subtract out the fact that people just differ from each other.

The problem with big individual differences

Suppose you test three conditions and people vary enormously to begin with: some always score high, some always low. In a between-subjects design, that person-to-person variability becomes noise that swamps the condition effect. You might have a perfectly real effect and still fail to find it, buried under "well, people are just different."

The repeated-measures trick

Because every person is measured in every condition, each person serves as their own control. The analysis can split off a "subjects" chunk of variation and set it aside, leaving a much cleaner comparison of the conditions. In the subject-centered view above, where each person's own average is subtracted out, the tangle of lines collapses into a clear shared pattern. That's exactly what repeated-measures ANOVA does mathematically. It's also the reason the honest picture of a within-subjects result is one line per person rather than a row of bars: the chart chooser calls that a slopegraph, and Tables & Figures That Don't Lie shows what goes wrong when you draw the bars instead.

The payoff is power. By removing stable individual differences from the error term, a repeated-measures design can detect a real effect with far fewer participants than a between-subjects design would need. Fewer people and more sensitivity, which is why the design is so popular.

Two conditions first: the difference score

With only two conditions there is no new machinery to learn, because repeated-measures ANOVA is the paired t-test wearing different notation. Write D = X2 − X1 for each person and the whole design collapses into one column of numbers. The null hypothesis becomes μD = 0, and a one-sample t on that column is the test. Two conditions are also where the reason pairing works is easiest to see, so it is worth a small example before the general table.

Five people did a reaction-time task twice, once on its own and once with a distracting second task running. Times are in milliseconds, so smaller is faster.

Every person got slower under the distractor, and four of the five by between six and nine milliseconds.

Why the difference column is so quiet

The reason sits in one identity from probability, which the course writes out because it is the whole justification for the design:

Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) − 2 · Cov(X, Y)
Cov(X, Y) = r · SD(X) · SD(Y)

Covariance is the unstandardized cousin of the correlation: the average product of the two variables' deviations from their own means, in whatever units they came in. Divide it by both standard deviations and you get r back. In the table above Var(single) = 616.0, Var(dual) = 669.0 and r = .998, so Cov = .998 × 24.82 × 25.87 = 640.5, and the identity gives

616.0 + 669.0 − 2(640.5) = 4.0,

which is exactly the 2.00² the difference column shows. That subtraction of twice the covariance is the entire mechanism. When people keep their rank order across conditions the covariance is nearly as large as the two variances, so almost all of the between-person variation cancels and what survives is the part that differs within a person. When the correlation is weak the covariance is small, little cancels, and the paired design has bought you nothing while still costing a degree of freedom. Around r = .5 the two designs break even, which is the number between vs within designs works through.

The repeated-measures table

With three or more conditions the difference-score trick runs out, because there is no single difference to take. What replaces it is a partition of the sums of squares that does the same job in a more general way. Start from the one-way table, where the total splits into a conditions part and everything else, then split that "everything else" once more:

SStotal = SSconditions + SSwithin subjects
SSwithin subjects = SSsubjects + SSerror

The second line is what a between-subjects design cannot do. Measuring each person once leaves no way to tell a stable personal level apart from a random wobble, so both stay inside one error term. Measuring each person k times separates them, and the subjects piece is then removed from the comparison rather than counted against it.

Each piece is a sum of squared deviations from a mean, and which mean is the only thing that changes. Writing n for the number of people, k for the number of conditions and N = nk for the number of observations:

SourceSum of squaresdf
Subjects (between people)SSS = k Σ (x̄person − x̄)²n − 1
ConditionsSSC = n Σ (x̄cond − x̄)²k − 1
Error (person × condition)SSE = Σ (x − x̄person − x̄cond + x̄)²(n − 1)(k − 1)
TotalSST = Σ (x − x̄)²N − 1

Two things about the error row are worth pausing on. Its formula is a residual: what is left in a score once its own person's level and its own condition's level have both been accounted for. And its degrees of freedom are a product rather than a subtraction, which is the same (n − 1)(k − 1) that an interaction gets in a two-way table. That is not a coincidence: the repeated-measures error is the person-by-condition interaction, the extent to which the conditions do different things to different people. Mean squares then follow the usual rule, MS = SS/df, and the test is

F = MSconditions / MSerror, on F(k − 1, (n − 1)(k − 1)).

The subjects row gets no F of its own in most reports. Its F would test whether people differ from each other, which nobody doubts and nobody set out to ask.

Now run the same 24 numbers as a one-way ANOVA that knows nothing about who produced them. SSC is unchanged at 1296, but the subjects row no longer exists, so its 10164 falls back into the error: 10220 on 21 degrees of freedom, MS = 486.67, and F(2, 21) = 1.33 with p = .29. Same data, same condition means, and one analysis reports an effect you could not miss while the other reports nothing at all. That is the picture the two bars in the widget draw, and pushing the "how different people are" slider widens the gap: the repeated-measures F does not move, because the slider adds variation to the subjects row and to nothing else, while the one-way F sinks as its error swallows the lot.

Drag that slider the other way instead, down to zero, and something worth knowing happens. The one-way F climbs to 243.00 and overtakes the repeated-measures one, which is still 162.00. With no individual differences left to remove, the subjects row is pure bookkeeping, and the seven degrees of freedom it took out of the error term would have been better left there: the one-way error gets 56 over 21 df where the repeated-measures error gets the same 56 over 14. A within-subjects design pays only when people genuinely differ from each other, and the more they differ the larger the payment. This is the same bargain a second factor strikes in a factorial design, and it is why the correlation between conditions is the number to think about before choosing the design rather than after.

Reading the design back out of the df

A paper that reports F(3, 84) has told you more than it looks. The first number is k − 1, so there were four conditions. The second is (n − 1)(k − 1) = (n − 1) × 3 = 84, so n − 1 = 28 and the study ran 29 participants, who between them produced 116 observations. Exams ask this both ways round, and it is a fast sanity check on a paper: a repeated-measures error df that is not a multiple of k − 1 means something has been reported wrong, and a number that implies a strangely small n is worth a second look.

Mixed designs: one factor between, one within

Most real studies cannot make every factor within-subjects. You can measure the same person before and after training, but you cannot put them in both the training group and the control group. A mixed design takes the factors it can repeat and repeats them, and takes the ones it cannot and assigns people to them. One between-subjects factor crossed with one within-subjects factor is the commonest shape in psychology by a distance, and the language follows the split: a within-subjects factor is one whose levels every participant experiences, and a between-subjects factor is one whose levels different participants experience.

Say forty people are randomly assigned to a meditation course or a waiting list, twenty in each, and everyone is tested for attention at baseline, after four weeks and after twelve. Group is between, time is within, and the table splits in two, because the two factors are tested against different error terms. Group differences are differences between people, so they are judged against how much people differ; time differences happen inside a person, so they are judged against the much smaller person-by-time residual.

Here g is the number of groups, k the number of measurement occasions and N the number of participants rather than observations. The five rows add to 119, which is Nk − 1 for 120 observations. SPSS prints them as two separate tables, Tests of Between-Subjects Effects and Tests of Within-Subjects Effects, and the second is where both the time effect and the interaction live. Reading the error df backwards is the same trick as before with one extra step: 76 = 38 × 2 gives 38 = N − g, and the between-subjects error df of 38 with two groups says 40 people in total, not 40 per group.

The reading order is the one a two-way table uses. Look at Group × Time first, because it is usually the question the study was designed to answer: did the two groups change differently? If it is significant, the two main effects describe averages that may describe nobody, and each is reported as qualified by it. A main effect of time then means "averaging over both groups, scores moved", and a main effect of group means "averaging over all three occasions, one group scored higher", which in a pre-post study is often an artifact of a baseline difference rather than anything the treatment did.

What the design costs

Everything above is the upside. Measuring the same person repeatedly also creates problems a between-subjects design never has, and the course groups them under order effects: any change in the response that comes from a condition's position in the sequence rather than from the condition itself. Two of them push in opposite directions. Practice improves performance as people get used to the task, so whichever condition runs last looks best. Fatigue and boredom do the reverse, so whichever condition runs last looks worst. A carryover effect is the more specific problem of one condition still acting during the next: a drug not yet cleared, a strategy just learned, a mood the last block put someone in.

The standard defense is counterbalancing, running the conditions in different orders across participants so that a pure order effect falls equally on every condition and cancels in the means. With two conditions that is half the sample each way; with more, a Latin square gives each condition every position without needing all k! sequences. What counterbalancing does not do is remove the variability. A symmetric order effect stops biasing the condition means and stays in the error term, and an asymmetric one, where A changes B more than B changes A, is not balanced away at all. When the carryover is severe enough, the honest fix is a between-subjects design.

Three further costs are worth stating plainly, because they decide the design as often as power does. A participant who misses one condition is lost from every condition, since the table needs a complete row. The conditions have to be repeatable in the first place, which rules out anything that teaches, reveals or permanently changes something. And the analysis carries an extra assumption, sphericity, which the rest of this page is about.

The trade in one line: a within-subjects design buys power by removing stable individual differences from the error term, and pays for it with order effects, a stricter completeness requirement and an extra assumption. Reach for it when the conditions are reversible and people are consistent, counterbalance whenever you do, and go between-subjects when they are not.

The catch: sphericity

Repeated measures come with their own assumption, sphericity: roughly, that the differences between every pair of conditions have similar variance. When it's violated (common with time-based data, where adjacent timepoints correlate more than distant ones), the test becomes too liberal. Standard corrections (Greenhouse-Geisser, Huynh-Feldt) adjust the degrees of freedom to compensate.

What ε is, and what it costs

Both corrections work through a single number, epsilon, written ε. It measures how far the pattern of condition-to-condition differences has drifted from sphericity, and it runs from 1 down to a hard floor of 1/(k − 1). Perfect sphericity gives ε = 1. Three conditions can fall no lower than .5, four no lower than .33. Multiply both degrees of freedom by ε and re-read the F table; the F ratio itself never changes, only the distribution it is compared against.

Greenhouse-Geisser is known to under-estimate ε when the violation is mild, so Huynh-Feldt applies a correction to the correction (that .785 is computed from the .71, not measured separately) and can reach 1, at which point software caps it. The convention most textbooks give: take Greenhouse-Geisser when its ε falls below .75 and Huynh-Feldt when it does not. Report which one you used and print the ε beside it, because fractional degrees of freedom tell a reader that something was corrected without telling them by how much. The assumption-checks sheet lists this one alongside every other check a comparison of means owes you.

There is also a more modern route. A mixed (multilevel) model treats each participant as a random effect and estimates the correlation structure directly, so it needs no sphericity correction, tolerates unequal spacing between timepoints, and keeps a participant who missed one session instead of dropping them from every condition. The word mixed is doing different work there than it does in "mixed design" above: a mixed design is about which factors repeat, a mixed model is about fixed and random effects, and a mixed design is usually what a mixed model gets fitted to. Repeated-measures ANOVA is still the clearer way to understand what within-subjects designs buy you, which is why it comes first. When the repeated outcome is a category rather than a number, the same reasoning arrives as McNemar's test: pair each person with themselves and count only the ones who changed.

Why it matters: within-subjects designs are everywhere in psychology, medicine, and UX research, anywhere you can measure the same unit repeatedly. Knowing that they trade a sphericity assumption for a big gain in power helps you design more efficient studies. Plan My Analysis will lay one out for you, assumptions and write-up included.

Common questions

Why does partial eta-squared come out so large in a within-subjects study?

Because its denominator leaves out the very thing the design removed. Partial η² is SSeffect / (SSeffect + SSerror), and in a repeated-measures table the error excludes the subjects row entirely, so the number answers 'how much of what is left once people are accounted for' rather than 'how much of the variation in the data'. The eight people worked through above make the gap concrete: their partial η² is 1296/1352 = .96, while the same effect against the raw total is 1296/11516 = .11. Neither is wrong and they answer different questions, but they are not comparable across designs, which is why a within-subjects study routinely reports an effect size several times the size of a between-subjects one testing the same thing. Say which you computed, report the design beside it, and if you need a number a reader can hold against a between-subjects study, generalized η² is the version built for that comparison: it puts the subjects variance back into the denominator for any factor a participant did not choose.

Mauchly's test came back non-significant. Does that settle it?

Less than it looks. Mauchly's test is a hypothesis test like any other, so what it reports depends heavily on how many people you have. Simulated at 5,000 samples per row, with three conditions and a real violation of ε = .71, it rejects sphericity 53% of the time at 12 participants, 82% at 20 and 96% at 30. The damage the violation does is meanwhile flat: the uncorrected F ran between 6% and 7% false positives at every sample size tried, and the Greenhouse-Geisser correction returned it to 5% each time. Your sample size governs the test's verdict far more than the problem does. Since the correction costs almost nothing when sphericity does hold, the safer habit is to apply it from the estimated ε and report that value, rather than letting Mauchly decide.

What happens if a participant misses one condition?

A classical repeated-measures ANOVA drops that person from the analysis entirely, in every condition, because the computation needs a complete row per participant. Lose a handful of people that way and you can give back most of the power the design was chosen for. Two respectable routes out: a mixed model, which keeps every observation a person did provide, or a principled treatment of the missing values before the ANOVA. Substituting the person's own mean for the gap is not one of them, since it invents data with zero variance and shrinks your error term.
