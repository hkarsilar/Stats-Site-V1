Section 2.6

Non-Parametric Alternatives

When the assumptions behind a t-test or ANOVA don't hold — heavy skew, nasty outliers, tiny samples — you don't have to give up. Non-parametric tests throw away the raw numbers and work with their ranks instead. That one move makes them remarkably tough.

The trick: rank, don't measure

Most parametric tests care about how far apart values are. Rank-based tests only care about order — who's bigger than whom. Replace the numbers [2, 5, 9, 400] with their ranks [1, 2, 3, 4] and that monstrous 400 becomes just "the biggest one." Its ability to distort the result evaporates. That's why non-parametric tests are so robust to outliers and skew.

Notice the asymmetry. As the point rockets upward, it inflates group 2's mean and its variance at the same time, yanking the t-statistic around — sometimes the t-test even loses significance because the variance blew up. The Mann-Whitney test? The point was already the highest; making it higher changes no ranks, so its p-value holds rock-steady.

The non-parametric toolkit

For most parametric tests there's a rank-based counterpart:

  • Mann-Whitney U (a.k.a. Wilcoxon rank-sum) — replaces the independent-samples t-test.
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank — replaces the paired t-test.
  • Kruskal-Wallis — replaces one-way ANOVA.
  • Spearman's ρ — a rank-based correlation, robust to outliers and curved-but-monotonic relationships.

The trade-off

Robustness isn't free. When the data are well-behaved and normal, the parametric test has slightly more power — by discarding the magnitudes, rank tests leave a little information on the table. The rule of thumb: use parametric tests when assumptions hold, and reach for non-parametric ones when they clearly don't (small samples, heavy skew, ordinal data, or outliers you can't explain away).

Why it matters: real data is messy — Likert scales, reaction times, income, anything with a few extreme values. Non-parametric tests give you a trustworthy answer exactly when the textbook assumptions fall apart.
