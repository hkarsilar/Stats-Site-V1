Section 5.1

The IMRaD Structure

Almost every empirical paper — a lab report, a journal article, your thesis — is built from the same four blocks: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. IMRaD isn't an arbitrary template a committee invented; it's the order a reader needs to follow your reasoning. Learn what each section is for — one job per section — and most writing decisions ("where does this sentence go?") answer themselves.

Four sections, one job each

The whole structure fits in four sentences. If a paragraph you're writing doesn't do the job below, it's in the wrong section:

  • Introduction — why the study. Set up the problem, review what's known, find the gap, and end on your specific hypothesis or question. It moves from broad context to one narrow prediction (this is where your H₀ and H₁ come from).
  • Methods — what you did. Who took part, what you measured, and how you analysed it — in enough detail that a stranger could repeat the study. No results, no interpretation; just the recipe.
  • Results — what you found. The descriptives and test statistics, reported plainly with no spin. Numbers and what they say directly — not yet what they mean.
  • Discussion — what it means. Interpret the findings, connect them back to the literature, admit the limitations that matter, and point to what's next. This is the only section where you're allowed to editorialise.

The hourglass

IMRaD has a shape: an hourglass. The Introduction starts wide (the big topic everyone cares about) and narrows to your exact hypothesis. The Methods and Results are the tight neck — specific, concrete, about your study only. Then the Discussion widens back out, from your particular finding to what it means for the field. Start broad, squeeze to the specific, open back up.

Reading order ≠ writing order. Readers go top to bottom, but you shouldn't write that way. Draft the Methods and Results first — they're the most factual and the easiest to pin down. Then write the Introduction (now you know exactly what you're introducing) and the Discussion. The abstract comes dead last, once everything it summarises actually exists.

The classic misplacements

Graders see the same four mistakes every year, and every one is a section doing another section's job:

  • Interpretation in the Results. "This shows that napping boosts memory" is a claim — it belongs in the Discussion. Results just report the number.
  • Findings in the Discussion for the first time. Every result you interpret must have already appeared, with its statistics, in the Results section.
  • New literature in the conclusion. If a citation matters, introduce it in the Introduction — don't spring fresh sources at the end.
  • Method details in the Results. "We used a 40-item recall task" describes procedure; it lives in Methods, not next to the numbers.

The structure tells the story. Read the four sections in order and you get the whole argument in miniature: here's the question (I), here's how I looked (M), here's what I saw (R), here's what it means (D). If a reader gets lost, a sentence is almost always sitting in the wrong block — put it back and the flow returns.

Why it matters: IMRaD is a promise to your reader about where to find things. Keep the promise and a busy examiner can skim straight to your Methods to check your design, or your Results to check your numbers, without hunting. Break it — bury a finding in the Discussion, smuggle interpretation into the Results — and you make the reader do work that should have been yours. The best-organised paper is often the one that simply put every sentence in its right room.

Common questions

What does IMRaD stand for?

IMRaD is the standard structure of an empirical paper: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Each has exactly one job — the Introduction says why the study (context → gap → hypothesis), the Methods say what you did (in enough detail to replicate), the Results say what you found (the numbers, with no interpretation), and the Discussion says what it means (interpretation, limitations, and what's next). If a paragraph doesn't do the job of the section it's sitting in, it's in the wrong place. The lowercase 'a' is just 'and'.

Should I write a paper in the order it's read?

No — and trying to is why blank-page paralysis hits at the Introduction. Reading order isn't writing order. Draft the Methods and Results first, because they're the most factual and concrete: you already know what you did and what you found. Then write the Introduction (now you know precisely what you're setting up) and the Discussion (now you know what you're interpreting). The hypothesis in your Intro should match the analyses in your Results, which is far easier to guarantee if the Results already exist. Write the abstract dead last, once everything it summarises is finished.

Where do I put the interpretation of my results?

In the Discussion, never in the Results. The Results section reports the finding plainly — "the nap group recalled more word pairs, t(58) = 3.72, p < .001" — and stops there. Saying what that finding means ("this supports the idea that sleep aids consolidation"), how it fits prior work, or why it might have happened is interpretation, and interpretation lives in the Discussion. The mirror-image mistake is just as common: don't introduce a finding for the first time in the Discussion — everything you interpret there must already have appeared, with its statistics, in the Results.
